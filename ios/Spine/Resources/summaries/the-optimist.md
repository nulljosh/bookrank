# The Optimist

# Prologue: The Optimist — Sam Altman, OpenAI, and the Race to Invent the Future (Keach Hagey)

## Opening scene: Peter Thiel's birthday, November 2023
On a mid-November evening in 2023, Peter Thiel hosted a birthday party for his husband at YESS in LA. Seated with his friend Sam Altman, Thiel — an early Altman backer and mentor — warned him he wasn't taking board dysfunction seriously enough: "You don't understand how Eliezer has programmed half the people in your company to believe this stuff. You need to take this more seriously." Days later (five days after this dinner), OpenAI's board fired Altman, citing that he was "not consistently candid in his communications with the board." He was reinstated within days after nearly all 770 employees signed a petition threatening to quit for Microsoft.

## Effective altruism vs. Altman's Silicon Valley zeitgeist
Thiel viewed OpenAI as having been captured by "the EAs" (effective altruists) — utilitarians who'd pivoted from ending global poverty to preventing AI from destroying humanity. Thiel had predicted OpenAI would destroy the company since funding it as a nonprofit in 2015, correctly forecasting "seventeen of the last two financial crises." He saw Altman as a well-known catastrophist turned Silicon Valley darling.

Altman had made optimism his personal brand, capping a stellar 2023: ChatGPT (launched November 2022) hit 100 million users in under three months, the fastest-growing app in history. GPT-4 followed months later, passing the bar exam and AP biology test. Altman wasn't the engineer — he was the visionary, evangelizer, and dealmaker, embodying the Paul Graham-taught "add a zero" ethos: think bigger, always.

## OpenAI's unusual governance and the AI safety fear
OpenAI's nonprofit board had fiduciary duty to "humanity," not investors — a deliberate structure meant to guard against a race to unsafe AGI. The 2018 charter declared OpenAI would "stop competing with and start assisting" any competitor who got close to AGI first, out of fear of value-misaligned systems. This fear traced to the 2017 "AI Principles" conference (funded partly by Elon Musk and Skype's Jaan Tallinn) and Nick Bostrom's *Superintelligence*, plus Eliezer Yudkowsky's LessWrong community and his "paperclip maximizer" thought experiment. Ironically, board members who voted to oust Altman had ties to the EA community, not simple business disagreement — Thiel's read (that the board feared runaway AGI) turned out wrong; the real reasons involved distrust in Altman's candor and Y Combinator-style founder-power dynamics.

**Photo (cover):** Sam Altman looking upward, purple-toned cover of "The Optimist" by Keach Hagey.

## The interview at OpenAI HQ, eight months before the firing
Hagey (a Wall Street Journal reporter) interviewed Altman in March, mid-AI-fever, at OpenAI's unmarked San Francisco Mission District office (formerly a mayonnaise factory). Description of Altman: small, rail-thin, 5'8", intense green-eyed gaze, boyish despite being thirty-seven. He spoke about alignment, safety, and the "AI Manhattan Project" (a meeting title on his calendar). Notable quotes:
- "I was extremely fortunate that early in my career I made more money than I could ever need... I want to work on what I think is going to be interesting and important and useful and critical to get right, but I don't need more money."
- On global AGI governance: "You can't do it as a government project... a non-profit is a reasonable approach."
- On safe AGI: "The vast majority of people in the world are much better off than they would have been in a world where AGI just didn't happen... I don't think most people love their jobs."

OpenAI's office featured a cafeteria, 1980s living room with board games, a library replica inspired by Stanford's Green Library, and a signature winding central staircase Altman was proud of — described by the author as "geeking out about it like a priest in a temple."

## Altman's vision of AGI's effects
- Extension of human will/abilities: AI tutors smarter than any human today.
- Falling costs for goods/services (law, design, programming) as AI handles labor.
- Universal basic income (UBI) funded by robot-driven wealth.
- Government/private co-investment in data centers powered by nuclear fusion.
- Curing cancer, extending lifespan by 10 years, brain-computer implants.

His investment portfolio (400+ startups) reflects this: **Helion** (nuclear fusion, $375M+), **Oklo** (nuclear fission microreactor), **Worldcoin** (iris-scanning crypto/UBI orb). Paul Graham on Altman's investing: "I think his goal is to make the whole future."

**Photos in this chapter:** None beyond the cover photo.

## A second meeting: April 2024, Baccarat Hotel NYC
A year after the first interview and five months after the firing/reinstatement, Altman met Hagey again — warmer, more guarded about the book (he'd initially declined participation, calling a biography "too soon"). Key friction point: Altman said "I really object to the warping of history that comes when one person gets imputed onto a company, or a movement, or a tech revolution." He also downplayed OpenAI's progress as premature to celebrate: "OpenAI has a long way to go."

## Research scope and family background
Hagey conducted 250+ interviews with Altman's family, friends, teachers, mentors, co-founders, colleagues, investors, and portfolio companies. Family background: father Jerry Altman (real estate developer, political activist on affordable housing); mother Connie Gibstine ("science brain," fierce work ethic). Sister Annie cut off contact with the family in 2018 amid tension around Jerry's death. Raised Jewish in suburban St. Louis, attended progressive Central Reform Congregation and rigorous John Burroughs School, both instilling a sense that he had "a moral duty to improve the world." Came out as gay as a teenager via AOL Instant Messenger chats in the late 1990s.

## Career arc preview
- **Loopt** (Stanford undergrad startup, location-based social network for flip phones) — his only company before OpenAI, backed by Sequoia; sold for parts in 2012. Most important outcome: introduced Altman to Paul Graham.
- **Y Combinator**: Graham chose Altman as his 2014 successor to lead YC; under Altman, YC scaled to hundreds of startups/year and spun up a research lab (staffed partly by Greg Brockman, later OpenAI co-founder/former Stripe CTO).
- **OpenAI**: first commercial product to use a large language model at scale; ChatGPT and GPT-4 turned academic AI research into "one of the greatest startup stories of all time."

## Altman's philosophy and beliefs
Wary of discussing publicly, but sympathetic to the simulation hypothesis — that reality may be a simulation, a view Thiel shares ("Sam lands on the simulation side and I land on the non-simulation side"). Altman meditates, doesn't believe in a personal God, and embraces Advaita Vedanta (Hindu nondualism) — the idea that Brahman (universal consciousness) and Atman (individual self) are equivalent, and the world is illusory ("maya"). Tweeted after ChatGPT's release that he believes in the "absolute equivalence of brahman and atman." Told podcaster Lex Fridman: "I think it's interesting how much the Silicon Valley religion of the simulation has gotten close to Brahman." Summary line: "This is all a dream. And in the dream, anything is possible."

## Key takeaways
- The book frames Altman as a master dealmaker/storyteller more than a technologist — the "add a zero" Silicon Valley ethos personified.
- OpenAI's nonprofit-controlled-by-a-for-profit structure was designed explicitly to prevent an unsafe AGI race, but this very structure enabled the November 2023 boardroom coup.
- Effective altruism and AI-safety ideology (Yudkowsky, Bostrom, LessWrong) shaped OpenAI's founding charter and its internal politics far more than most outside observers realized.
- Altman's philosophy — belief in the simulation, Advaita Vedanta nondualism — colors his public confidence that "anything is possible," tying his metaphysics directly to his AGI optimism.
- Since his reinstatement (the "blip"), Altman has pushed OpenAI toward a more conventional for-profit structure, potentially holding up to $10 billion in equity, and become the undisputed face of the AI industry — while facing lawsuits from Elon Musk accusing him of betraying OpenAI's original nonprofit mission.

# Chapter 1: Chicago

## Harold Washington's inauguration, April 29, 1983
Chicago's first Black mayor, Harold Washington, was inaugurated on Navy Pier amid chants of "We want Harold!" He'd beaten both the incumbent mayor and Mayor Richard J. Daley's son in the Democratic primary, overcoming an opponent's racist campaign slogan ("Epton for Mayor, Before It's Too Late") and a coalition backlash. A young community organizer named Barack Obama, who tried to work with Washington, later echoed his line: "Politics ain't beanbag." Washington called his win "the result of the greatest grassroots effort in the history of the City of Chicago."

## Jerry Altman (Sam Altman's father)
Watching proudly from the audience was **Jerry Altman**, then in his early thirties — a real estate consultant working on affordable housing, and one of Washington's few white supporters in city government. Jerry had helped pioneer using the federal Low-Income Housing Tax Credit to finance affordable housing, convincing skeptical business interests it was a smart investment, not a handout. He would later co-found a real estate firm and remain civically engaged around housing policy for decades.

## Family background: the Altmans and Gibstines
- **Jack Altman** (Sam's grandfather) was a decorated WWII Army Corps veteran who married Sam's grandmother Selma; the family ran a shoe company and jewelry/import business in St. Louis, building modest wealth as Eastern European Jewish immigrants.
- Jerry Altman grew up comfortable in St. Louis, attended St. Louis Country Day School, was involved in progressive politics from a young age, and eventually moved to Chicago after law school, drawn to Mayor Washington's coalition politics and housing reform work.
- **Connie Gibstine** (Sam's mother) came from a Hartford, Connecticut Jewish family; her father Marvin Gibstine was a St. Louis-born, Harvard-educated doctor and real estate investor; her mother Judy ran a decorating business. Connie attended Washington University in St. Louis, earned a medical degree in 1986, and became a dermatologist.
- Jerry and Connie met in Clayton, Missouri (an affluent St. Louis suburb) and married; they briefly lived in Chicago for Jerry's housing-policy work before returning to Clayton by the time their third child, Jack, was ten weeks old — driven partly by rising Chicago property taxes and city inconveniences that frustrated Connie.

## Samuel Harris Altman's birth
**Sam Altman was born April 22, 1985** in St. Louis. As an infant he had colic, waking constantly for the first few months — described as an unusually intense baby from the very start. He was the oldest of four children: Sam, Max, Annie, and Jack.

## Growing up in Clayton, Missouri
The Altmans settled in an affluent, historically Jewish-friendly St. Louis suburb (Clayton), known for excellent public schools. Jerry worked in commercial real estate/affordable housing consulting; Connie built her dermatology practice. The family was "unapologetically WASPy... in an assimilated Jewish way," per a family friend — active but not deeply religious, prioritizing education and civic engagement. The children grew up with private-school-quality public education, encouraged toward independence and ambition.

**Photos in this chapter:** None (all text pages).

## Key takeaways
- Sam Altman's parents were both civically engaged professionals in real estate/housing policy and medicine, instilling early exposure to policy, deal-making, and ambition.
- The family's roots trace to Eastern European Jewish immigrants who built modest wealth in St. Louis over generations.
- Jerry Altman's affordable-housing finance work (Low-Income Housing Tax Credit) reflects a family pattern of pragmatic, deal-driven approaches to social goals — a dynamic echoed later in Sam's own blend of idealism and dealmaking at OpenAI.
- Sam was born into a stable, high-achieving Midwest household in Clayton, MO, setting up the chapters that follow on his childhood there before Chicago (revisited) and eventually Silicon Valley.

# Chapter 2: St. Louis

## Hillcrest neighborhood, Clayton
The Altmans settled in a brown-brick Tudor in Clayton's Hillcrest neighborhood — a charming, prewar, architecturally distinct area on the city's edge near Forest Park (bigger than Central Park) with a hole in the fence leading to a top-rated local elementary school. Sam later recalled "petrichor" (the smell after rain) as his favorite smell, tying it to childhood memories of the Missouri suburbs. The family emphasized to their children that they "could do anything," though early tensions existed: Connie had dreamed of starting her own dermatology practice since Chicago but found the prospect overwhelming with four young kids at home, so she instead joined a group practice.

## Connie's career and community roles
Connie became a well-regarded dermatologist in St. Louis. Jerry continued in commercial real estate and affordable housing consulting, and was deeply active in the community: he served as president of the Reform congregation's board, and was later "part-decided" involvement with Democratic and community-development groups, including a stint chairing a Community Development Corp. Family Thanksgivings and Passovers were large gatherings; Sam described his father as "gregarious" and social, in contrast to his own more reserved nature.

## Early intellect and technology
Sam's parents recall him being unusually verbally and intellectually precocious — reading, using complex vocabulary, and displaying computer proficiency far ahead of peers. His third-grade teacher taught him BASIC programming; he was given a Mac at age 8 and quickly began teaching himself more advanced material. His parents noted that if he wanted to learn something, he'd immerse himself and often surpass what they could teach him.

## John Burroughs School
Sam attended John Burroughs School, a private, prestigious St. Louis prep school with an unusually strong emphasis on independent thought, community-oriented education, and progressive values ("we don't know how to do this," a philosophy that empowered students). He was in one of the school's first classes to have consistent access to a computer lab. Teachers and classmates recalled him as intensely curious, already interested in AI concepts, philosophy, and computer science by early teens — reading Kurt Vonnegut and other big-idea fiction, and a "philosophical Norse Star" among students.

## Coming out and identity
Sam came out as gay via AOL Instant Messenger conversations as a teenager in the late 1990s — a way to explore identity somewhat anonymously online before doing so in person. His parents recalled some early anxiety about how this would be received socially but ultimately were supportive; Connie described feeling initially conflicted imagining her "gay, Jewish son at a big Southern state school" (with computer science just starting there) — "I felt like that was punishing him for being so good, and so diligent" — so she and Jerry told him to follow his own path rather than a conventional big-state-school computer science track.

## Social life and friend circles
Sam became increasingly social and popular by high school despite being younger and unconventional; he joined the debate team, ran with a "Coffee Cartel" friend group that hung out at cafés, and dated his first boyfriend, Nathan Watters, in the LGBTQ community at his high school — notably, Burroughs had one of the most visible/accepted queer social circles among ~200 all-boys-and-girls prep peers at the time (unusual for St. Louis in the early 2000s). Some classmates recall him as ambitious and precociously confident about his future even then, telling people he wanted to change the world.

## College search and Stanford
Sam applied and was accepted to Stanford, drawn by its computer science program and Silicon Valley proximity. His parents supported the choice despite the financial stretch of sending four kids to private school while maintaining their Clayton home and mortgage.

**Photos in this chapter:** None (all text pages).

## Key takeaways
- Sam Altman's precocious intellect and early access to computers (BASIC at age 8, personal Mac) set him apart academically well before Stanford.
- John Burroughs School's progressive, independent-thought-driven education directly shaped his confident, unconventional problem-solving style.
- Coming out as a teenager via AOL Instant Messenger reflects both the era's nascent digital identity exploration and Burroughs' relatively accepting environment.
- Family support — particularly Connie's choice to let him pursue Stanford CS over a "safer" path — set up the next chapter's transition to Silicon Valley and Loopt.

# Chapter 3: "Where Are You?"

## Stanford, April 2005

The chapter opens on Saturday, April 9, 2005 at Stanford — a rare rainy April on the Peninsula, the campus quiet and hungover after a night of Greek Row parties. In FroSoCo (Freshman Sophomore College), the party-free dorm housing for nerds at the western edge of campus, eighteen-year-old sophomore **Sam Altman** wakes up clear-headed in the bed of his boyfriend, **Nick Sivo**, and begins what would become the most consequential thirty-six hours of his life.

## Meeting Nick Sivo

Altman and Sivo met almost immediately on arriving at Stanford the previous school year, in the auditorium-style lecture hall of **CS106X** — Stanford's accelerated single-quarter combination of its introductory computer science courses CS106A and CS106B.

Sivo's background mirrors Altman's in several ways:
- Eagle Scout and valedictorian of his Texas high school
- Raised in the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints
- Mother a doctor (a radiologist), like Altman's; father a CPA working as CFO for a software consultancy
- Grew up in a house full of computers, first got online through his bulletin-board–running father

Altman's own early-tech biography is recapped: teaching himself programming, the Mac SE/30 and later machines, learning to show off what a computer could do, and being the kid with a soldering iron and a Compaq TC1000 tablet — a self-described combination of impatience and almost unimaginable ambition.

## Looking for a problem worth solving

Back on campus in the sophomore year, Altman circles among friends — Sheila Deshpande and others in the engineering-school orbit — trying to find something to build. He is drawn to the idea that phones, not desktops, are where the next platform lives.

Key insight of the chapter: **location**. Altman notices that every phone knows roughly where it is, and that no one is using it. The obvious answer to the question friends text each other constantly — *"Where are you?"* — could be answered automatically by the device. The FCC's E911 mandate (requiring carriers to be able to locate phones for emergency services) meant the capability was already built into the network; it just wasn't exposed to users. Privacy advocates (EFF, the Electronic Frontier Foundation) had already flagged the surveillance implications, and Altman was aware of them.

This becomes **Loopt** — a location-sharing service for mobile phones, effectively a "social network with a location component."

## The BASES competition and early money

Altman and Deshpande enter the Stanford **BASES** (Business Association of Stanford Entrepreneurial Students) business-plan competition. Deshpande drops out before the pitch; Altman takes the stage alone in a wood-paneled room. The judges — venture people and executives, including **Patrick Chung** (then at New Enterprise Associates, one of America's largest venture firms) — are convinced. Chung, a Trinidad-born, Taiwan-raised physicist-turned-Harvard-and-Oxford product of an immigrant family, is struck by how much older than his years Altman seems.

The critical practical problem: Loopt could not work without **carrier deals**. Access to handset location data meant signing agreements with the carriers themselves — Sprint, Verizon, AT&T/Cingular — the slowest, most bureaucratic counterparties in the industry. Chung's advice, and Altman's own instinct, was to go straight at the CTO level rather than through business development.

## Y Combinator's Summer Founders Program

Meanwhile **Paul Graham** — Cornell- and Harvard-trained, Viaweb founder, essayist whose posts had already made him a cult figure among young programmers — announces Y Combinator's **Summer Founders Program** for summer 2005: small checks (on the order of $6,000 per founder), a summer in Cambridge, and access to a network. Graham's essays, not the money, were the draw; Altman had read them and was already inside that world.

Altman had a competing offer: a prestigious summer research internship at Stanford, plus a standing option from the Cambridge computer-science track. He picked YC, replying to Graham with characteristic bluntness and rescheduling to make the interview work.

**Bill Gates cameo**: on the same trip window, Altman recalls waiting in a room where Gates went by — "the interview only lasted twenty-five minutes," Altman noted, and he remembered thinking *"Ah, so this is what smart people are like."*

## The 36 hours

The chapter's framing device — the consequential day and a half — resolves in a phone call: that evening, Altman received a call from Graham on his cell phone saying Loopt had been accepted, and the startup was funded by Y Combinator.

## Why it mattered

Beyond Loopt itself, the acceptance put Altman inside the network that would define the rest of his career:
- He met the investor who would not only back Loopt but help convince **Sequoia Capital** to invest — a relationship that would last for decades.
- He became part of Silicon Valley's most important alumni network — the one that would, more than a decade later, give birth to "a small but audacious research project called **OpenAI**."

## Photos in this chapter
No photographs or figures appear in these pages — body text only, with running heads ("THE OPTIMIST" / "WHERE ARE YOU?") and margin annotations from the reader.

## Key takeaways

1. Loopt was born from a mundane question — "Where are you?" — plus a technical capability (E911 location data) that already existed but was unexposed to users.
2. The idea's fatal dependency was carrier partnerships, not technology; Altman's early lesson was to go over the heads of business-development gatekeepers straight to CTOs.
3. Altman chose the Y Combinator network over a prestigious research internship — an early example of optimizing for access and optionality rather than credentials.
4. The relationships formed in that summer (Paul Graham, Sequoia, the YC alumni network) are the direct through-line to OpenAI a decade later.
5. Sivo was both Altman's boyfriend and his technical co-founder — the personal and professional entanglement runs through the entire Loopt story.

# Chapter 4: Among the "Nerd's Nerds"

## Paul Graham, in person

The chapter opens with a portrait that undercuts the stereotype: **Paul Graham doesn't look like a nerd.** Handsome, sturdy, boyish even into middle age — sandy brown hair, deep dimples, a fondness for preppy polo shirts that make him look, from the waist up, like the sort of golf-playing bank executive his own essays make clear he has nothing but disdain for. Below the waist he is famous for rumpled cargo shorts and sandals — the ultimate signal of having exited one's startup successfully enough never to have to impress anyone again. His underlings gave him an autographed pair of the shorts as a parting gift when he retired from YC.

## Graham's background

- Son of a British mathematician who worked on nuclear reactors, ran more than eighty marathons, and wrote books about chess in his spare time.
- Born in England, raised in **Pittsburgh**, his father chasing nuclear jobs across the Northern Hemisphere as regulators slowly strangled the industry.
- Self-described "bad kid": *"Half the kids in my neighborhood were forbidden to play with me… I was suspended from school I think at least once a year from first grade until twelfth grade."*
- Computers weren't really part of school; he began to code at **fifteen**.
- **Cornell** (started in philosophy, switched to artificial intelligence), then **Harvard** for a PhD in computer science, then still more school — this time for **painting** — and a move to New York City to be, in his words, a "starving artist."

## Viaweb and the essays

Graham co-founded **Viaweb** (with Robert Morris and Trevor Blackwell), an early web-based store builder — arguably the first true SaaS application — which **Yahoo bought for about $49 million** in 1998. Morris and Blackwell each contributed technical firepower; Graham's contribution was equal parts code and ideas.

He then became famous for **writing**. Starting around 2001 he wrote and published essays on his own website: *"Gradually it dawned on me that the most important thing about Viaweb was not the software but the ideas."* The best-known are collected in **Hackers & Painters** (2004), whose title essay argues that hackers and painters are makers, not scientists — that programming, like painting, is something you learn by doing and judge by beauty. *"Great software requires a fanatical devotion to beauty."*

Graham's rule of thumb, quoted repeatedly: pay attention to what smart people are actually building, and back the people, not the plan.

## The founding of Y Combinator

In **March 2005**, Graham accepted an invitation to speak to the **Harvard Computer Society**, choosing the topic *"How to Start a Startup."* It became a pep talk for a generation of ambitious young men. Instead of just talking, he decided to fund. With **Jessica Livingston** (whom he was dating and later married), Morris, and Blackwell, he launched **Y Combinator**.

**Jessica Livingston's background**: daughter of a New England fishmonger; grew up in Andover, Massachusetts; Wellesley College; began her career at the Gillette Company and later at **Adams Harkness & Hill**, where she did marketing for the Boston investment bank. She was, in Graham's telling, the one who could read people — the "social radar" of the partnership. Her book *Founders at Work* came out of the same instinct.

The insight Graham had to convince Livingston of: **venture capital was mispriced at the seed stage.** VCs are "intelligently cowardly" — they hunt for other VCs' validation. The main problem, in Graham's view, was that VCs were paid a percentage of the amount of money they managed, so they had every incentive to write big checks and none to write small ones. Livingston's objection was that giving young programmers a few thousand dollars each wasn't a business. Graham's answer: the point was to become the seed-stage brand, the "newscaster" that non-VC founders trusted — *"They tried to find our own business-minded CFOs, people Graham dubbed 'balancing scooters'"* — and once you own deal flow at the bottom, everything else follows.

## The Summer Founders Program, 2005

Graham originally floated the idea as a problem to solve for himself: teach twenty-somethings how to start companies, in batch form, over a summer.

**The batch dinner.** Heading into the first Tuesday-night group dinner, Altman was one of 135 applicants; the group Graham assembled was famously young and famously unpolished. Around YC's Cambridge headquarters — a converted candy factory — Graham and Blackwell built a version of what would become a Silicon Valley institution: office hours, weekly dinners, Demo Day, and relentless iteration on the pitch.

**Altman among them.** The chapter's set piece is Altman at that first dinner, holding a stack of "candy-bar"-style **Sony Ericsson** and Motorola Razr flip phones, pulling them out of his pockets and explaining that each ran on a different network, required a different carrier deal, and needed a different application for each. He was carrying the complexity of the mobile ecosystem in his jacket. Graham's reaction afterward: *"After that conversation I thought he was like a genius."* Gurevich said, *"He was a little off, speaking-wise. I was like, 'Maybe this guy's on the spectrum.' But listen, so was Aaron, and so were a couple of the other guys."*

Altman did not, in fact, get testicular cancer — but he did make the cut. Graham's verdict at nineteen: *"His startup felt more actualized"* than the others'. Huffman agreed: *"Fundraising from mad VCs? That wasn't going to work for those of us doing this. We didn't know how to talk to them, didn't know the game. In fact, Paul only kind of knew how to talk to them."*

## The rest of the batch

The Summer Founders Program is presented as one of the most consequential cohorts in tech history:

| Founder(s) | Startup | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Steve Huffman, Alexis Ohanian | Reddit (pivoted from **MyMobileMenu** / "Snarfer" food-ordering idea) | Graham told them to abandon it and build a front page for the internet; sold to Condé Nast (~$10M) in Oct 2006 |
| Sam Altman, Nick Sivo | **Radiate** (later renamed **Loopt**) | Location sharing; the biggest fundraiser of the batch, and the startup Graham considered the most promising of the year |
| Justin Kan, Emmett Shear | Kiko (calendar app) | Sold on **eBay for $250,000**; proceeds + Graham seed → **Justin.tv** → **Twitch**, bought by Amazon for nearly **$1 billion** in 2014 |
| Phil Yuen | TextPayMe | Sold to Amazon |
| Zak Stone | — | Went on to work for **Google Brain** |
| Aaron Gurevich | ClickFacts | Sold for ~$160,000 — "10X" back to YC; *"think of all the cup ramen you could buy!"* |

The book flags the irony: in "an utterly surreal turn of events proving either the incestuousness of Silicon Valley, the absolute hegemony of the YC alumni network, or both," the board that fired Altman years later hired **Emmett Shear**, however briefly, to be his replacement.

## Loopt's carrier war

Back on Loopt's actual business: the network had its roots in some of America's oldest infrastructure — telegraph lines that once ran along railroads, the Southern Pacific Railroad's lines becoming **Sprint** (Southern Pacific Railroad Internal Networking Telephony). Altman made repeated pilgrimages to **Sprint's headquarters** in Overland Park, Kansas — a 200-acre corporate campus of red brick, buildings, and parking structures built in the mid-1990s at a cost of some **$3 billion**, complete with amphitheater and its own street network, one of the largest building projects in United States history.

The technical prize: **carriers control location data.** Altman met with **Sprint's CTO**, Chung, and others, flying back and forth from Cambridge to St. Louis and Kansas City. Sprint eventually agreed to a deal on Loopt's terms — including the crucial concession that Loopt could send a photo to anyone in the user's address book, and that Sprint would charge **$2.99 per month** per subscriber, splitting the revenue.

Lee began to take Loopt to annual pilgrimages to the Alibaba district in central Tokyo, home to Harajuku girls photographing themselves with flip phones — a preview of where mobile social was heading. Japan's girls taking pictures using the flip phone was, to Loopt, proof the future had already arrived somewhere.

**The privacy problem.** One lunch, Altman was asked what Loopt would do if a mobile carrier provided location data the wrong way. His answer: *"We thought, 'Well, this owner might be worried about privacy.'"* The service needed to take a user's approval to locate them; Loopt built consent and granular sharing controls in from the start — an approach that made the company slower to grow but far more defensible.

## Fundraising

Graham connected Altman to **Chung** at NEA, and Altman returned to Cambridge determined to close a round. He diligently attended the Tuesday dinners, worked the network (Steve Wozniak among the encounters), and turned Loopt into the batch's standout raise. **Redditt/Reddit** had built a user base over the summer, but Altman was determined to be the biggest: he proved YC's model by taking the seats fifteen investors had filled. Livingston wrote: *"They ended up with about fifteen investors and each of them was a Mac-a-Vac"* — and the determination that Altman was **"the most businesslike person there."**

Reddit had built a user base over the summer, but it was Altman who kept a screenshot of the pitch. **Kiko** had the most promise as an idea, but Altman had the most business sense. He was the youngest recipient of a Mad-Ave "genius grant" among about fifteen investors, beginning with an NEA-led round. **Sequoia** ultimately led, and Loopt became by far the best-funded startup of the batch — reportedly around **$5 million**, an enormous sum for a nineteen-year-old in 2005, and later far more.

*"We contacted every rich person we knew in the area and I was praying that Graham originally really had a problem to invest,"* Graham wrote of that period. *"They ended up giving about fifteen investors and each of them was a Mac-a-Vac."*

## Key takeaways

1. **Graham's arbitrage was structural**: VCs were paid on assets under management, so nobody wanted to write small checks. YC bought the entire seed stage cheap by being first and being a brand.
2. **YC's real product was the network and the essays**, not the ~$6,000 checks — reputation and deal flow compounded from day one.
3. The **Summer 2005 batch** (Reddit, Loopt, Kiko→Justin.tv→Twitch) is arguably the most consequential cohort in startup history, and its alumni network still governs the industry — including, eventually, Altman's own firing and replacement.
4. **Altman was the batch's most "businesslike" founder** at nineteen: he was the one who could negotiate with Sprint's CTO and raise the largest round, not the one with the best product.
5. Loopt's dependency on **carrier deals** was both its moat and its cage — it took years of trips to Overland Park to unlock what the iPhone would later make free for everyone.
6. Graham's favorite of the batch was Altman's **Radiate** — the company that would be renamed Loopt and ultimately return the least of the cohort's headline exits. The model worked because the batch, not the judgment, produced the outliers.

# Chapter 5: "Stopping Out"

## Fall 2005 — the decision not to go back

As fall approached, the three co-founders prepared for their junior year at Stanford. **Sivo** had signed up as a resident computer coordinator and moved into campus housing. **Altman** got an apartment off-campus in Mountain View. **Deshpande** was back from India, and they had all registered for courses.

But Altman's heart wasn't in it. He had returned from Cambridge a changed person, filled with the confidence PG — as everyone called Graham — had breathed into him. He even had his own PG-style, email-handle nickname: **sama**.

Stanford's euphemism for taking indefinite leave was **"stopping out"** — not dropping out, just pausing. None of the three ever went back.

## The first term sheet — August 31, 2005

**Radiate** (the company that would become Loopt) got its first term sheet from **NEA**: **$1.6 million, valuing the company at $6 million.** The teenagers needed a lawyer.

### Page Mailliard and Wilson Sonsini

Altman reached out to **Page Mailliard**, an attorney at **Wilson Sonsini Goodrich & Rosati** — the most powerful law firm in Silicon Valley, with an office near the Stanford campus. Led by the quiet but fearsome **Larry Sonsini**, consigliere to everyone from Steve Jobs to Google founders Larry Page and Sergey Brin, Wilson Sonsini was the preferred firm for startups raising money from the venture capital firms on Sand Hill Road.

Mailliard — platinum blond hair, bright blue eyes — had been at the firm since the 1980s, "when Palo Alto was Podunk," she said. She studied English at Stanford, then earned her JD from Harvard Law School. *"Law is all about language. It's about people and language."*

She loved both people and language, and she especially liked the three Stanford students who walked into her office in jeans and T-shirts on a September day asking for help with the NEA paperwork. Mailliard's assessment of Altman in particular: he listened to their story, asked the right questions, and — unusually for a founder that age — grasped the details of what he was agreeing to.

She offered to introduce them to **Greg McAdoo** at **Sequoia Capital**.

## Greg McAdoo and Sequoia

**Greg McAdoo** grew up on Manhattan, the son of a Black civil-rights activist and academic who had once recorded an album with Pete Seeger. He studied at **Cornell University**, where he ran the department at Storm Book University. Once, when he was fined, he responded that in order to get out of the ticket he'd have to learn to fly. From that moment onward, McAdoo had been obsessed with airplanes. He wrote software that included cumulative and open-flight aviation training. His first computer was a TRS-80, and he taught himself to write in exchange for writing some accounting software for his uncle's clinic. After initially working for Adobe, he received a private pilot's license and worked for a time flying tube-based bulk-air-quality equipment. McAdoo had been in a fine-tuned data pilot didn't pay very well. His last stop before Sequoia was **Sierra Wireless** in San Francisco, where he was an executive. *"Being an engineer did..."* — SourceCom, which McAdoo saw it all.

**Sequoia Capital was founded in 1972 by Don Valentine**, one of the pioneers of Silicon Valley venture. His view: *"My prediction has always been you find a great market and you build multiple companies in that market."* He explained: *"So far no co-founders pointed to the importance of personal networks. It was through a Fairchild connection that he learned about Atari, which became Sequoia's first investment. And it was through Atari, where Steve Jobs worked as an engineer, that he learned about Apple and came to sit on its board."*

By the time McAdoo was ready to leave **Cisco in 2000**, Sequoia would split a **$5 million** investment and get half the company back in return, including about **$50,000 from friends and family**.

## The competitive dynamic with YouTube

The chapter draws the contrast that would define Radiate's fate:

> In comparison, **YouTube**, which Sequoia invested in out of the same fund, had given up just **30 percent** of its equity to Sequoia in a seed round and Series A. At roughly the same size that valued the online video portal at **$15 million** — a far more typical percentage to give up at such an early stage. In some ways the difference reflected the gap between Radiate's theoretically enormous potential and its ability to show any evidence of it in that moment: what YouTube could show was actual user traction, because a platform with no carrier gatekeepers, no privacy consents, and no per-device app builds could grow at internet speed. Radiate would have to strike deals with telecom companies before it could even have users.

Meanwhile Sequoia's incubation space within the same building — a modest, dun-colored building that looked like a public library — is where Radiate shared an office with the **Web 1.0 payments company PayPal**. Another former PayPal employee was the Sequoia partner who led the fund's investment into **YouTube**. Sequoia, and veterans of the Web 1.0 generation, invested **$5.15 million in Radiate**. Within a year, **Google would purchase YouTube for $1.65 billion** — one of the Radiate employees would later describe the memory of hacking alongside the YouTube founders as **"humbling."**

### At first, though, the co-founders worked out of Sequoia's headquarters on Sand Hill Road

The Radiate and YouTube teams were evidence of a great thawing in Silicon Valley, after a period of frozen capital following the bursting of the dot-com bubble. *"It was just the beginning of people starting to look at new ideas that maybe didn't have great ideas, and they were beginning to see this again,"* one recalled. *"They tried to look at more ideas than most companies did. They didn't have revenue, and people had to have some vision to see this and feel it was worthwhile after having been burned through the years."*

## Radiate leases its own office

**Radiate leased offices in a modest two-story tan building at 850 Alma Street in Palo Alto**, near the busy intersection of El Camino Real and Page Mill Road, taking distance from the Stanford campus. The primary feature was a spiral staircase that jutted dangerously into the main room. *"They tried to keep getting attention and would smack their foreheads walking into it,"* recalled Nitin Tang, the company's office manager. *"It was a hazard!"* There was a **Ping-Pong table** and a **flat-screen TV**. The Foosball tables were the norm.

*"I primarily used for playing Halo 3. The Foosball brothers brought their guitars and their French bulldog,"* and the engineers routinely coded through the night and slept on the office floor.

## The pitch, and what the company was really about

Altman's actual sales pitch was about **"enhancing serendipity."** He spoke about how, in college, he would often have free time and want to find his friends on campus before class. When he later explained the pitch to his mother, she replied: *"Oh, this is a cure for loneliness, right?"*

Altman and everyone he hired **shared a common belief in the power of mobile devices to bring people together in the real world, rather than merely entertain them while they were on the toilet or waiting in line at the bank.**

## Adult supervision: hiring Jacobstein

Unlike Marciniak, **Jacobstein's** hiring was what McAdoo called **"vocational"** — meaning he was *"brought in to fill some gaps for Sam."* As Jacobstein put it: *"I was supposed to be the Sheryl to his Zuck"* — referring to the long-successful relationship between Facebook COO **Sheryl Sandberg** and CEO **Mark Zuckerberg**, fifteen years her junior.

Jacobstein was given the title of **executive vice president, corporate development and marketing**, which meant he was there to chaperone the still-teenaged Altman through meetings where he might have trouble being taken seriously — such as with wireless carriers and regulators.

### The privacy strategy that prefigured OpenAI

Together they came up with an approach to trying to allay the privacy concerns around their app: **aggressively reaching out to anyone who might plausibly complain** — the **Electronic Frontier Foundation**, the **ACLU**, battered women's shelters, the **National Center for Missing & Exploited Children** — and bringing them in on design decisions. *"By involving them in the process, it helped ease their concerns,"* Jacobstein said.

The book flags this explicitly as formative:

> It was a lesson that Altman would absorb and apply to remarkable effect at OpenAI. In the meantime, when it came to landing deals with wireless carriers, it wasn't clear that Altman really needed that much help.

## Key takeaways

1. **"Stopping out"** — none of the three co-founders ever returned to Stanford; the leave was permanent in practice from the first term sheet.
2. Radiate gave up an unusually **large share of equity** (Sequoia + NEA taking far more than YouTube's ~30%) because it had potential but no traction — the price of a business gated by carrier deals.
3. The **YouTube contrast is the chapter's engine**: same fund, same building, same year — one company grew at internet speed and sold for $1.65B; the other negotiated with telecoms.
4. **Altman's product thesis was social, not technical**: mobile as a cure for loneliness, "enhancing serendipity," phones bringing people together in physical space.
5. **The privacy playbook — co-opting your critics by putting them in the design room (EFF, ACLU, NCMEC) — is the direct ancestor of Altman's OpenAI strategy.**
6. Altman was surrounded early by **adult supervision by design** (Mailliard on legal, McAdoo on the board, Jacobstein as the "Sheryl to his Zuck") — a pattern that recurs across his career.

# Chapter 6: "Where You At?"

## Boost Mobile — the first real customer

Founded in **Australia in 2000** as a brand aimed at the country's surfing-obsessed youth, **Boost** was the first "virtual" mobile network — known in the industry as a **mobile virtual network operator (MVNO)** — to run atop **Nextel's** network. Nextel had decided early on to carve out a niche among truckers and thus pushed all of its phone-makers to put **GPS chips** in their phones. Boost set up offices in **Irvine, California**, a center of American surf culture, and began marketing its prepaid service to a mix of young surfers and urban youth.

> *"A lot of young, urban kids couldn't get contracts, so they needed prepaid devices,"* said **Lowell Winer**, director of innovation and business development at Boost at the time. *"Frankly, a lot of drug dealers used Boost, because there was no contract. There was no name on it."*

To attract a young demographic, Boost hired a roster of surfers, BMX riders, MCs, rappers, and UFC fighters to spread its message. Sometimes they would show up on the second floor of Boost's strip-mall-style offices on Friday afternoons, when beer was served, and at least once someone rode a motocross bike up the office stairs. It was all a stark contrast to the Kansas City headquarters of Boost's parent company, **Sprint**, which Boost employees referred to as **"Shawshank."**

By **early 2006**, Winer was convinced that Boost needed to use its GPS capabilities to differentiate itself from the competition, and had put out a request for proposals for an idea that sounded a lot like Radiate: **a "friend finder" that would let you know when your friends are close by.**

## Altman's negotiating lesson

The Boost deal is where Altman learned a fundamental lesson: **patience is leverage.** He never came clearly to his own well-defined negotiating positions — he'd simply outlast the other side.

> *"Anyone who came across him at that time wished they had quite quickly,"* one colleague put it. *"He was decisive and optimistic. Rarely if he was skeptical about anything."*

The dynamic in the room: Winer's team would offer a deal, Altman would go quiet, and the terms would move. He didn't need to bluff. He simply wouldn't sign.

## The Boost deal and the Sprint conflict

Boost's deal was so fundamental to Radiate's future that they **decided to rename the company**. Boost had decided to keep the company's "health mode," declining to put out a press release about the Series A, knowing that it would take time to land the wireless deals and build the technology that was needed. They toyed with **"Flipt"** but ran into trademark issues. Facebook was just beginning to spread, and the name they eventually settled on was **Loopt**.

The problem: the Boost deal ran through **Sprint**, and Sprint's own separate deal covered other services. The tile-building for Boost meant Loopt's "Social networking as a hot space and people are looking to fit in the mobile space," he told **Yahoo!** (and eventually, in **HBO's satirical Silicon Valley**, the identical sounded line as an extension of its closest indispensable partner, Boost). Boost kicked around a bunch of names and settled on one that would change from Radiate to Loopt.

The only key persons missing from the map, besides Sivo, was **Boost's director of marketing**, who had joined the little-known Sprint entity — and the compromise reached was that Loopt could keep both Sprint and Boost as customers, provided the Boost product remained visibly branded as Boost's own.

## Loopt launches — November 2006

**Altman launched his first product in Times Square** as strangely surrounded by rappers, and Loopt itself launched **in November 2006**, surrounded by rappers, jeans, and a hoodie with a cartoon Boost logo. The temporary stage erected in the middle of the plaza carried a live map of Manhattan with little colored dots that showed his and his colleagues' locations. Behind them, a phone's tiny gridded map was blown up on a humongous backdrop to convey the product's features. The screen showed a Times Square view of the product's core promise: **the phone knows where your friends are.**

The launch snapshot: near the same-day launch, Boost was "the biggest carrier that could produce something and something at the same time." A **Sprint** version launched shortly after, and Loopt had **35,000 users** by the release.

## The business reality: carriers as gatekeepers

The chapter's core argument is that **Loopt's entire fate ran through the carriers, and the carriers were not built for speed**:

- The only way to reach a mass audience in 2006 was to get preinstalled on handsets, or listed in a carrier's on-deck app catalog.
- Every carrier — Sprint, Verizon, AT&T, T-Mobile, Cingular — required a separate deal, a separate build, separate revenue splits, and separate approvals.
- Carriers moved on multi-quarter timelines and had no incentive to make any single app succeed.

**Altman predicted Loopt's deal with Boost led to more marriage with carriers**, and by the fall of 2006 turned to **Winer, David Wodlen** (the carrier's advisor with industry insider connections), and others. Altman was booked to speak with the **Wall Street Journal**, and **Wodlen had a direct line into Cingular's CEO**, who would soon become part of AT&T. Wodlen had a direct line into Cingular's CEO. *"So the point,"* Altman said, *"is to put Altman in front of the most important executive at AT&T."*

**Boost's marketing campaign** helped propel Loopt to more than **50,000 users** within the first three months of its launch. By mid-2007, Loopt had graduated from the "sub-brand" of Boost to its own first-tier deals, and it was clear that Loopt had graduated to being one of the applications the carriers were beginning to notice.

## The privacy fight, part two

Loopt's biggest structural risk was **regulation**. The chapter details Altman's Washington education:

**Sprint's legal team was brutal.** *"I recalled Winer. Altman flew to Sprint, Virginia, the home of Nextel, and sat with Winer across the table."* Legal was able to fix the company's assent as a *"soberly rigid, unfriendly, gruff, so far as no one else's assets"* — Altman was given an extremely narrow window in which to make his case. *"What is he for making a mistake?"* Altman said.

Altman agreed to some concessions:
- **The privacy aspect of things** was a hard "no longer trust outside their frame" arrangement.
- Users would have to **opt in explicitly**, twice, before anyone could see their location — a design decision that cost Loopt growth but made the regulatory case defensible.
- For the first two weeks, reminding users would get flexed at them, and the registration process would involve scrolling through repeated privacy notices and disclaimers. That was enough for Boost.

Still, **Jacobstein deported Loopt with great personal misgivings.** He appeared before the **Congressional Internet Caucus**, where the sponsoring firms told him he would be sponsoring a "Wild West" — he became the *de facto* consent to the people of Altman.

The chapter records the deeper irony: **Loopt built the entire privacy apparatus that the industry later abandoned wholesale.** Loopt's disclosures, opt-ins, and outreach to advocacy groups became the template, and then the iPhone arrived and made all of it moot.

## The team, and the beginnings of friction

There was plenty to be stressed about. For all of Altman's pitch prowess, the company was finding it difficult to bring on more wireless carriers. Some of their meetings suffered from inside-baseball tension — *"Hey, that's really interesting,"* they'd say, and then nothing.

The strain shows up in the portrait of **Jacobstein**, who by the end of the chapter was privately uneasy:

> One scene remains fixed in his mind. About a year into his business, Jacobstein asked Altman what he was thinking about besides Loopt. Altman had two answers: **a cure for baldness, and nuclear fusion.** Jacobstein scoffed to himself at the time: *"What do you know about fusion? You're a nineteen-year-old sophomore computer science dropout."*

But Altman could think about anything — anything at all, even the technology stack. Jacobstein realized that if Altman could make one of the handful of nuclear-fusion startups outside of Loopt work, it wouldn't matter that Altman had ever run a company.

*"There's a blessing between 'I think I can maybe accomplish this thing but I have already accomplished this thing that is going to be hard to sell,'"* Jacobstein recalled. *"That is really a healthy example of this random management approach."* The board, he added, would not have unfolded that Altman was pressing for Jacobstein to replace Altman as CEO. Those complaints kept coming, and it was hard on employees; the board should hear complaints and told them to get back to work.

## Key takeaways

1. **Boost Mobile** — an MVNO on Nextel's GPS-heavy network aimed at surfers and urban youth — was Loopt's first real customer, and its RFP for a "friend finder" was effectively Loopt's product spec.
2. The company **renamed itself from Radiate to Loopt** around the Boost deal (after trademark trouble with "Flipt").
3. **Altman's negotiating style was patience, not aggression**: he'd go quiet and let the other side move.
4. **Carriers were the whole business and the whole problem** — every one required its own deal, build, and revenue split, and none moved fast.
5. Loopt built the industry's most careful **privacy regime** (double opt-in, disclosures, advocacy-group outreach, congressional testimony) — and then the smartphone era discarded that standard entirely.
6. **Growth was real but small**: 35,000 users at launch, 50,000+ within three months — impressive for carrier-gated 2006, invisible next to what was coming.
7. Even at nineteen, Altman's actual interests were already elsewhere: **a cure for baldness and nuclear fusion** — an early tell that Loopt was never the ceiling of his ambition.

# Chapter 7: From "Weak" to "Cool"

## Little did Sam Altman know that the entire premise of his company depended on striking a deal with Steve Jobs

The chapter's opening line is its thesis. Loopt was a product dependent on carriers — and Apple was about to take the carriers' power away.

## Steve Jobs and the iPhone

Before Jobs unveiled the **iPhone** at Macworld in **January 2007**, phone makers were the ones on the road at Apple's beck and call, and the powerful wireless networks — **Cingular, Verizon, Sprint, T-Mobile** — told them what to build and decided what applications would come preinstalled. The phones were often free when you signed a contract with a wireless carrier.

Jobs changed that dynamic. *"We kept a walled garden,"* recalled the chapter's account of Apple's early plans: Apple focused on its **exclusive deal with Cingular** (which later became AT&T) in exchange for **five years of exclusivity for the iPhone**. Jobs changed the phone's economics — but the wireless carriers no longer decided which applications a user could install.

**Except that, at first, no one could install anything at all.** When Jobs launched the iPhone in June 2007, there was **no SDK and no App Store**. Developers were told to build **web apps**: *"a beautiful and rich set of applications"* accessible through **Safari**. Jobs's line at the time — the one that infuriated an entire industry — was that web apps were all developers needed.

### The "sweet solution" that wasn't

Jobs "casually announced" what he called a *"sweet solution"* — the iPhone's web-app strategy — to a Worldwide Developers Conference audience that had been waiting for real native development. Developers could build web apps on Apple's Safari browser. Jobs was uncompromising: no native code, no SDK, no store.

Apple's stance would not hold. Developers jailbroke the device within weeks; the pressure became unbearable; and Apple reversed course.

## Loopt's problem

Loopt had built its entire business on **location APIs the carriers controlled**. If the iPhone made carriers irrelevant, then Loopt's hard-won, multi-year carrier deals — the very moat Altman had spent his teens building in Overland Park and Irvine — became worthless. But if Loopt could not get onto the iPhone, it would be locked out of the only phone that mattered.

**Howard Nicholson** (Loopt's engineering lead in these pages) *"wrote inside apps as we had using this time until Apple released a true SDK. Loopt-service: He was hiding his time until Apple released a true SDK."*

The interim workaround: Loopt built a web version that ran inside Safari, and Howard built himself and the servers that Loopt ran its system on those were the ones before the widespread adoption of the cloud platforms like **Amazon Web Services**, and ran the site in his spare time.

## The SDK meetings — November 2007

Apple announced the **iPhone SDK** in October 2007 and shipped it in **March 2008**.

Loopt was one of a small number of developers Apple invited in early — but **under crushing secrecy**:

- The **meetings in November 2007** were when Loopt saw the SDK, and Apple's NDA was so restrictive that the team could take **no detailed notes**.
- Everything went through Apple: *"If you sign an NDA you can't even talk about the fact you signed the NDA."*
- **Scott Forstall**, who led iPhone software, wanted to immediately push developers to build native apps.
- Loopt received a cryptic email from Apple, and product and engineering teams loved it.
- The team recalls being told the company was *"not going to be launched with the summer secrecy."* Altman posted a note to himself, and shook his head.

**Mortiz, taking a note from Valentine, said: "It's a race."**

The chapter frames this as Altman's first encounter with a platform owner who held total power — a lesson that would matter enormously later, when Altman himself became the platform.

## The App Store launch — July 2008

To get up on the coveted stage of **Apple's developer conference**, Loopt needed a demo that would make the iPhone look indispensable.

Altman had pitched himself as Loopt's demo. He was young. He was cheap. And he was, as the running joke went, "weak."

### The demo

The presentation itself is the chapter's set piece:

- Altman took the stage at the **Moscone Center, San Francisco**, in a neon-pink polo shirt with a second, neon-green polo underneath — an outfit remembered by everyone as *"the peak of a Boost."* This is the best version of Loopt that we have ever made, and Altman said as he was preparing to unveil.
- He had the opportunity to work with what Apple had as-yet unseen: the ability to show off the specific features of the new native app.
- He picked the one native app: **the ability to pinch the touchscreen to zoom out of a map and also launch it and then the app with a single click.**
- His demonstration showed a few friends nearby and pins on the map, and particularly close at a cafe site had previously nagged as *"the cutest cafe."*
- The presentation concluded with a note that at launch **Loopt would be free to iPhone users**: *"Loopt was so valuable to sign a bit later. You never have to eat lunch alone again, or it as bad. Loopt would be free to iPhone users."*
- *"To show off the iPhone's location capabilities that Apple even paid for a national television commercial showing how Loopt could be used."*

**His June 2008 presentation would make him a household name in the tech world.** He'd also something of a laughingstock in an interview with the chapter title's framing: *"weak"* was the word he'd used about himself. But that image, and the perception the bit book fashion choice of the world did not quite get to know his name, they'd at least now know the **"double-popped collar guy."**

> **Inside Apple, Loopt was an unqualified hit. Downloads surged.**

## The Apple deal and the aftermath

Altman prepared to launch the iPhone version of Loopt's location app. Apple wasted no time offering assurances — but the months with June Jobs were still coming. Given the deal, Apple **announced the iPhone would carry Loopt as a featured app**.

**Michael Moritz** (Sequoia) and **Greg McAdoo** disagreed about what came next. McAdoo said, *"Just the hardest thing to do."* Steve threw a jest at his best. Both McAdoo and Chung recall Altman shifting them "Steve threw a jest at his best, hard-headed."

Moritz's view of Altman: *"There was something about his intensity. You don't let go of great human beings you are people."* McAdoo said, *"You don't let go of an entrepreneur and a leader of high-performance people. That's not a thing. Half of the folks the greatly increased has as a result of watching from the inside out how Apple worked in the early years of the iPhone."*

**But the timing could not have been worse.**

## The 2008 crash and the plateau

> *"Loopt was almost downed up for content. That fall of 2008, after Lehman Brothers collapsed, things went worse. Social media coverage collapsed. That fall of 2008 after portfolio companies together Sequoia had gathered the CEOs of its portfolio companies together for an emergency meeting on Sand Hill Road and told them the era of easy venture money for smaller companies who want to alter their trajectory."*

The famous Sequoia **"R.I.P. Good Times"** deck landed exactly as Loopt was finally winning.

### The funding round that showed the ceiling

- **By the spring of 2009**, Loopt had landed a funding round valuing the company at **$150 million** — the same as it had been the depths of the financial crisis. The **$7.5 million round** was led by **DAG Ventures**, a middle-stage investment fund that frequently invested alongside Sequoia and continued some favorable terms for **DAG** that would allow them a larger share of the proceeds from a future sale. Sequoia and NEA optionally came along for the ride.
- **Earlier in the year**, and just before the wheels had come off the good economy, **Facebook had made a verbal offer to buy Loopt for around $150 million** — more than three times what it would eventually sell for.
- Sequoia's **Michael Moritz** had added a heavy dose of realism about it. Altman said he was going to pass. He wanted to build a bigger company.
- **Moritz's line, quoted as the chapter's hinge:** *"But as investors in November 2008, it was making a large enough point pitching a huge market for a network that is far from established"* — **"Beat 'Come on."**
- Years later, **Moritz spoke by the advice he gave Altman**: *"There was a moment in Loopt's trajectory where the future looked very, very promising, and therefore an early sale would have seemed premature,"* he said. *"Even though investors frequently get furnished with the benefit of wanting to bail out early, in my experience, it's one of the founders who want to do that (than certainly the long-term investors)."*

**Altman, in passing, had passed Sequoia's most important exit.**

## Key takeaways

1. **The iPhone destroyed Loopt's moat and created its opportunity at the same time.** Carrier deals — Altman's hardest-won asset — became worthless the moment Apple took distribution away from the carriers.
2. **Jobs's initial "sweet solution" (web apps only) was a dead end**, and Apple's reversal into the SDK and App Store in 2007–2008 is what saved developers like Loopt.
3. **Apple's NDA regime was absolute** — no notes, no acknowledgment the NDA existed. Altman's first lesson in what it means to be at the mercy of a platform owner, and a template for how he'd later run one.
4. **The WWDC/App Store launch demo in 2008 made Altman famous** — the double-popped-collar demo, an Apple national TV ad featuring Loopt, and surging downloads.
5. **Timing killed it**: the App Store win arrived just as Lehman collapsed and Sequoia declared the end of easy money.
6. **Altman turned down a ~$150M Facebook offer** on Moritz's encouragement, believing Loopt could be much bigger. Loopt eventually sold for roughly a third of that — the single most consequential "no" of his early career.
7. The chapter's arc is the title: Loopt went from **"weak"** to **"cool"** — and then the market closed the window.
