## Introduction

A recession is officially "a decline in GDP lasting two or more consecutive quarters," but the book's real focus is what you do about it regardless of the official label. Four objectives frame the whole book:
- Prepare yourself in case you lose your job.
- Take control of your finances — live within your means and build savings.
- Continue to enjoy your life while maintaining financial control (don't just deprive yourself — spend smarter).
- Have a plan for worst-case scenarios like bankruptcy or foreclosure.

The book is organized as standalone numbered tips you can read in any order — jump to what's relevant via the Table of Contents or index rather than reading cover to cover.

## Part 1: Facing Off Against a Recession

### #1 — Determining Your Net Worth
Net worth = assets − liabilities. Use the worksheet categories: cash accounts (checking, savings, money market, cash on hand), personal use assets (residence, vehicles, personal property), investment assets (retirement accounts, brokerage, mutual funds, other), against liabilities (mortgage, home equity loans/lines, auto loans, investment/margin loans, credit card debt, other loans). Update this figure periodically — it's your financial starting point and the way to track progress.

### #2 — Calculating Spendable Income
Spendable income ≠ gross salary. Start from gross income per pay period, subtract taxes (federal, state, local/city, FICA) and automatic payroll deductions (401(k)/retirement, health/dental/disability/life insurance, HSA/FSA contributions, union dues, stock purchase plans), then multiply by pay periods per year to get annual spendable income.

### #3 — Calculating Required Expenses
Required expenses = the bills that don't stop regardless of income: protection (health, life, disability, renter's/homeowner's insurance), housing (rent/mortgage, utilities, phone/internet), basic food and hygiene, and minimum debt payments. Distinguish these from discretionary spending — this is the "floor" you must cover before anything else.

### #4 — Calculating Discretionary Spending
Discretionary categories include entertainment, clothing/personal care beyond basics, gifts, dining out, subscriptions, and "other" (auto/parking beyond commuting need, misc.). Total discretionary spending against your after-tax income minus required expenses reveals your surplus — or shortfall. If there's a shortfall, this table shows exactly where to trim first.

### #5 — Tracking Expenses
Track spending for at least a couple of months before drawing conclusions — a short window rarely reflects true patterns. Any tracking method works (notebook, spreadsheet, app) as long as you're consistent; the value is in seeing where money actually goes versus where you think it goes.

### #6 — Setting Spending Practices
Long-term financial planning starts with defining your dreams (retirement, education, big purchases) and converting them into concrete goals using the "SMART" approach — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound. Steps: (1) brainstorm dreams, (2) determine which dreams matter most, (3) develop an action plan with a timeline and priority order.

### #7 — Creating a Budget
Three-step process:
- **Step 1 — Categorize your expenses**: group into medical/insurance, housing, food, transportation, personal, entertainment, gifts/donations, education, and savings/retirement.
- **Step 2 — Estimate what you spend**: for each category, tally recent statements/receipts.
- **Step 3 — Calculate and adjust**: compare income to spending; if expenses exceed income, cut discretionary categories first and set a target savings percentage.

### #8 — Breathing Away Tension
Simple breathing techniques for acute financial stress: breathe through your nose (slower, calmer than mouth-breathing), diaphragmatic breathing (hand on chest, hand on belly — only the belly hand should move), and "the quickie" — 3 slow breaths held a few seconds each, usable anywhere including mid-crisis.

### #9 — Tensing Your Way to Relaxation
Progressive muscle relaxation: tense a muscle group hard for ~10 seconds, then release and notice the contrast. Work through body parts sequentially (feet, legs, abdomen, chest, arms, hands, face) or use the "quickie" version — tense everything at once for 5 seconds, release, repeat.

### #10 — Stretching Away Your Stress
Target common stress-holding areas — neck, shoulders, back, jaw — with short stretches held 15-30 seconds. Gentle range-of-motion neck rolls, shoulder shrugs/rolls, and stretching hands/wrists after screen-heavy stretches of work.

### #11 — Practicing Habits of Effective Stress Managers
Recurring traits: exercise regularly, sleep enough, engage in a hobby, maintain a strong support system (friends/family/community — don't isolate under stress), stay organized (clutter and disorganization compound stress), manage time deliberately (calendars, lists), live according to your own values rather than others' expectations, and keep a sense of humor.

## Part 2: Bumping Up Your Bring-Home

### #12 — Updating Your Resume
Build a **core resume** first — a base document with contact info, summary, key skills, education, experience, activities/affiliations, honors — then customize ("spin off") targeted versions per job application rather than rewriting from scratch each time. Gather source material first: past resumes, employment records, training certificates, recommendation letters, project docs, and publications.

### #13 — Choosing the Right Resume Format
Three formats:
| Situation | Suggested format |
|---|---|
| Perfect career progression | Reverse chronological |
| New graduate | Chrono-functional |
| Seasoned professional | Reverse chronological, hybrid if gaps |
| Military transition | Reverse chronological or chrono-functional |
| Job history gaps | Chrono-functional or hybrid |
| Career change | Hybrid, sometimes reverse chronological |
| Demotions | Any |

Avoid a purely **functional** format (skills-only, no company names/dates) — it's a red flag to employers and should never be used.

### #14 — Making Your Resume Stand Out
- **Sell it, don't tell it**: replace "Responsible for X" with results — quantify impact, answer the implicit "so what?"
- **Use keywords**: employers and applicant tracking systems search for job-specific keywords (skills, certifications, tools, big-name employers) — tailor keywords per job ad rather than reusing a generic list.
- **Drop articles** (a, an, the) and "I" — resumes read as compact phrases, not full sentences.
- **Delete the leave-outs**: no "Objective" header, no "References available on request," no SSN/DOB, no old dates (10-15+ years), no irrelevant activities (political/religious affiliations, anything that invites bias).

### #15 — Preparing for a Job Interview
Don't over-script answers — sound natural, not memorized. Prepare: appropriate attire (one notch above the job's dress code), answers to "Tell me about yourself" (start from your first career-relevant moment, not birth), 2-3 talking points that differentiate you, and three ready PAR (Problem-Approach-Result) stories, 30-60 seconds each, drawn from work, school, or extracurriculars.

### #16 — Standing Out During an Interview
Good posture, firm handshake, genuine smile. Listen more than you talk — the "traffic light rule": first 30 seconds they're fully engaged (green), next 30 seconds attention starts to wane (yellow), past 1 minute you've likely said enough (red) — stop or ask a question. For panel interviews, address the asker first, then briefly meet others' eyes before returning. Prepare a few smart questions for when they ask if you have any — e.g., "What would you want me to accomplish in the first month?"

### #17 — Dealing with Video Interviews
Send materials in advance, test tech beforehand, frame the camera head-and-shoulders with no shadows, keep movements calm, look near the camera lens (not the screen) for eye contact, and treat the sign-off like a real handshake — say something warm and let the interviewer end the call first. Some interviews are now asynchronous (prerecorded questions, recorded answers) — same prep applies.

### #18 — Employing Post-Interview Tactics
Skip the generic thank-you note in favor of an **influencing letter**: reiterate enthusiasm, add new information you didn't get to mention, take a second pass at a question you fumbled, and optionally attach relevant collateral (writing samples, articles, references). Follow up a few days after the promised decision date if you haven't heard back — reaching out shows genuine interest without seeming desperate, as long as you don't do it too soon.

### #19 — Trying Quick Ways to Find Good Jobs
**Call-email-call**: leave a voicemail, immediately follow with an email, then call again a few days later — works even for cold outreach to employers not actively advertising. Aim broadly (10-20 target employers); rejection is expected and fine. **Phone a friend**: your network is more persuasive by voice than by text/email — call (don't just message) a dozen or so contacts who could plausibly know of an opening.

### #20 — Looking into Federal Jobs / Blast Your Social Media
Unless you're a born entrepreneur, consider government work: as companies and nonprofits convert ever more full-time jobs into freelance gigs, government is a major source of stable, benefited, full-time careers (paid holidays and vacations, typically a 40-hour week). The number-one way to spot federal openings is the official **USAJOBS.gov** site — search by keyword and location (or remote). Also push your search through LinkedIn and other social networks: connect with organizations and people in your target field, engage authentically, then ask for referrals once you've built visibility.

### #21 — Checking Out Best Career-Changing Tips
If your existing skills have grown obsolete or your field is shrinking, retraining into a growing occupation may be worth the time and money.
- **Change by retraining** — community colleges and certificate programs offer lower-cost, faster paths than a full degree; some employers subsidize it.
- **Use your network** — especially for people who lack the time/money for long retraining: an insider referral into a growing field can substitute for formal credentials.
- **Work up from the bottom** — take an entry-level job in the new field and learn on the job; don't let ego stop you if the long-term trajectory is good.

### #22 — Figuring Out Whether You Should Change Your Career
A career tweak isn't always the answer — sometimes unhappiness stems from the job or boss, not the career. A true/false self-assessment to gauge your unhappiness's real source (e.g., "You're confident that any wrong you see in your workplace is due to the specific job, not the whole field," "You know that changing careers is expensive and risky," "You've researched what the new career actually pays and demands"). Diagnose before you leap.

### #23 — Telecommuting as an Alternative to Traditional Work
Technology and communications software make remote arrangements viable for many jobs; studies show telecommuters are often *more* productive than in-office peers, and it can cut commute time, office costs, and stress. **Downsides:** monitoring performance is harder, scheduling coordination suffers, some equipment/security needs shift to the employee, and managers must be more organized. Depending on the role, employees can telecommute full-time, part-time, or occasionally.

### #24 — Persuading Your Organization to Let You Work from Home
Frame the request around **the company's goals**, not your convenience: show how remote work helps the org reach its specific objectives, do a cost/benefit analysis comparing what you save the company vs. any cost, and demonstrate that telecommuting will spend more of your time on actual work. "Coach yourself" — anticipate and pre-empt management's objections (visibility, collaboration, discipline).

### #25 — Considering Self-Employment
Being your own boss is an attractive option if it suits you. Ask honestly whether you have the attributes:
- **A self-starter** who doesn't need external motivation.
- **Ethical** — your reputation *is* your business.
- **Able to handle diverse problems** on the fly, since you wear every hat.
Self-employment income comes irregularly; make sure you (and your family) can weather lean stretches, and know that many ventures take a couple of years to become profitable.

### #26 — Getting Acquainted with Home-Based Business
A home-based business offers flexibility and low overhead, but carries real risks:
- **You're the boss** — self-discipline and drive are everything when no one's watching.
- **Health insurance may be unaffordable or unavailable** as a small business owner — a critical consideration.
- **You may fail** — most small businesses do; if you take on personal debt or guarantees, business failure can mean personal bankruptcy. Go in with open eyes.

### #27 — Setting Up Your Home-Based Business
Do the groundwork before you launch:
- **Develop a business plan** — clarify goals, honestly assess whether it can be profitable, and map who your customers are.
- **Consult outside professionals** — a lawyer, an accountant, and a banker can spot problems early.
- **Choose a legal structure** — sole proprietorship (simplest/cheapest but no liability protection), partnership, or corporation/LLC (limits personal liability, more paperwork).
- **Decide on a name** — pick something enduring; register it and protect the logo; check availability.
- **Take care of the red tape** — local/state/federal licenses, permits, and registrations vary by business type.
- **Get the insurance you need** — health, liability, property, workers' comp, business-interruption, etc.
- **Decide on an accounting system** — single- vs. double-entry, cash vs. accrual; use software (QuickBooks-type) to stay organized.
- **Develop a marketing plan** — know your target customers and how to reach them; treat it as a living document.

### #28 — Taking a Few Steps Before Leaving Your Job
Once your side business consistently earns enough to cover bare-minimum living costs *and* business expenses, you're ready to go full-time — but do these before you resign:
- **Check when company benefit plans vest** — a 401(k) may not be fully vested until a set number of years of service; check the vesting schedule before picking a resignation date, since leaving weeks early can forfeit real money (80–100 percent of your retirement match).
- **Find out when bonuses or profit sharing pay out** — annual bonuses/profit sharing are often slated for a month after the fiscal year ends; timing your exit around it can be worth a lot.
- **Get all annual health exams, procedures, and prescriptions done** while you're still covered. Check whether group coverage can convert to an individual policy at favorable rates, and if you're in the US, you're likely covered by COBRA (`irs.gov/general/topic/health-plans/cobra`).
- **Pay off or pay down credit card balances while you still have a steady job** — protects your credit rating and preserves financing options for the new business.
- **Don't announce or submit the resignation until you're actually ready to go.** Some companies are (sometimes justifiably) paranoid about departing employees taking ideas, proprietary data, or clients — you may be walked out immediately with a personal escort.

### #29 — Getting the Cash Flowing
Every new business starts at the beginning — cash flow is the primary early goal, and the faster you get it flowing, the sooner the business becomes self-sustaining.
- **Begin part-time with your new business** — keep your regular job for as long as you can while building. If the business fails, you still have income.
- **Work part-time at your old job** — if your home-based business has built up enough to keep you busy but not fully, negotiate part-time at the existing employer instead of a clean break.
- **Turn your employer into your first client.** Nobody knows your work better. Note the distinction: doing the work *as an independent contractor* rather than an employee — make that clear in the contract, or the IRS may disallow tax deductions you take for your home-based business.
- **Take business with you (ethically).** Stealing clients from a previous employer is unethical and may land you in court; an employer's blessing, in writing, is worth the effort to get.
- **Finance your business with startup funds** — lining up sources of startup money eases the financial strain of owning your own business.
- **Piggyback with your partner.** If a partner keeps their regular job, their paycheck and benefits can cover overall household income while you crank up sales — a shelter that saves a lot of sleepless nights.

### #30 — Surveying Tax Deductions Available to Home-Based Businesses
Home-based businesses pay self-employment tax (the government's way of collecting Social Security and Medicare from the self-employed) plus income, excise, state, and federal property taxes — 30 to 50 percent or more of revenue can go to taxes. Deductions are the counterweight.
- **Home-office deduction** — often a major financial incentive to start a business at home; it can literally mean the difference between success and failure. It lets you deduct the costs of operating and maintaining the part of your home used for business, whether that's a single-family home, condo, commercial building, or houseboat, provided you meet the IRS's criteria. The rules are complicated and the penalties for getting it wrong are significant — **consult an accountant or tax planner before taking it**. See IRS Publication 587, *Business Use of Your Home* (`irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p587.pdf`).
- **Other important deductions** — postage, auto expenses, internet access, business meals and entertainment, interest payments on business credit cards, health insurance, office supplies, furniture. To be deductible, the Internal Revenue Code requires an expense be **ordinary and necessary** for the operation of your business — a vintage 1959 sunburst Gibson Les Paul electric guitar is ordinary and necessary for a professional musician, and would surely sink like a lead balloon for a home-based software designer.

### #31 — Recognizing Often Overlooked Ways to Save on Your Taxes
The tax code is complex enough that hiring a professional tax lawyer or accountant usually pays for itself. Commonly overlooked strategies:
- **Maximize your vehicle deduction** — choose between the standard mileage deduction and actual costs (fuel, maintenance, taxes, license fees). Note that if you use the actual-cost method in the first year, you must stick with it ever after; and unless you use a vehicle 100 percent for business, keep a log of business use including dates, mileage, and purpose. See `irs.gov/taxtopics/tc510`.
- **Make the most of hiring employees** — consider hiring a family member. A child in a lower tax bracket lets you keep income in the family, and depending on how much the child earns, no tax at all may be owed on their earnings.
- **Reduce your taxes with a retirement plan** — money into a retirement fund reduces Adjusted Gross Income, which is what you use to calculate other itemized deductions. The self-employed can invest more using SEPs and SIMPLE Plans than with an IRA. See IRS Publication 560 (`irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p560.pdf`).
- **Make the most of a bad year** — consult a tax professional on whether you're better off taking an immediate tax refund or carrying the loss into your next business year; tax law on handling losses changes frequently.
It's against the law to *evade* taxes, but it's your duty to avoid paying more than you're legally obligated to — the IRS isn't going to volunteer special privileges for paying more than you have to.

### #32 — Ensuring the Survival of Your Home-Based Business in Tough Times
Good planning can't see far enough out to anticipate every shortfall, so build habits that let you weather each bump and emerge stronger.
- **Save for a rainy day.** When times are good it's tempting to run out and buy furniture, computers, the latest software. Instead set aside cash in a money-market fund to carry the business through a rough patch. Build a **cash reserve sufficient to run the business for a minimum of three months, preferably six to twelve**. After that's established and funded, go buy the fun stuff.
- **Manage your cash flow.** Maintaining *positive* cash flow is by far the number-one financial issue facing every small business owner. If you rely 100 percent on the proceeds of your home-based business to support you and your family — with no regular job bringing healthcare and other essential benefits — a shortfall in cash can quickly bring financial disaster. Keep an eye on net cash flow, be proactive about bringing cash in, and pay your bills only when they're due, but do so in time to avoid interest and penalties.
- **Keep in touch with your customers.** When times get tough, your first priority should be ensuring current customer relationships are *solid*: drop in for a visit, schedule a lunch, send an email, do whatever you can to keep your relationship on the front burner. And while you're busy keeping your relationship active, let your customers know that you're actively seeking more work — this gentle reminder that you're out there often leads customers to send more work your way, which is exactly what you need when times are tough.
- **Push your clients to pay their bills.** Monitor *receivables* — the money owed to your company by clients and customers. Most business accounting programs have built-in *aging reports* showing who owes you money, how much, when it's due, and how late. When a payment is overdue, act immediately, especially when the amount owed is substantial. Some tried-and-true collection tactics: call or visit the customer and ask for payment; offer to help; and **as a last resort — and only if you aren't concerned about getting any future business from that client — turn the debt over to a collections agency.** Whatever you do, when a payment is late, act immediately; don't wait days, weeks, or months hoping it'll come in.
- **Minimize expenses.** There are only two ways to hunker down: increase the money coming in, or decrease the money going out (or both). Minimizing expenses is the quickest of the two. Before you buy anything, ask whether you can survive without it for a while. Can the expense be deferred for a few days, weeks, or a few months? Can you barter services or borrow/rent equipment instead of purchasing it? Be careful, however, about exactly what expenses you cut — cutting the wrong ones can bring significantly more expense back into the business.
- **Offer a special promotion.** To quickly generate more business, offer a special offer for a limited time — a 10-percent discount on all orders during July, or a two-for-one. Alternatively, offer a value-added product or service: e.g., a gift certificate for $10 of merchandise to every customer who places an order of at least $100, or a free video. Offering a premium during a specific period keeps customers from expecting it forever.
- **Refuse to give up.** When times are tough, you may be tempted to throw in the towel. But what's much more rewarding — and ultimately much more challenging — is to fight for your business and refuse to give up, *no matter what*. When interviewed and surveyed, successful entrepreneurs most consistently attribute their good fortune to *persistence*. You can fail only if you allow yourself to do so.

### #33 — Boosting Your Household Income with a Side Hustle
When you can't make ends meet with expense cuts alone, look at the other end of the cash flow equation: more income. A side hustle is an activity you're *really, really interested in doing* — not your full-time, salaried, career-oriented job — that's on the side of your primary income and requires more than a minimal amount of time and energy (at least as you get established). Search "best side hustles" or "side hustle ideas" and you'll wind up with hundreds or thousands of results, many containing dozens of ideas — how do you find the needle in that information overload? Follow a methodical, step-by-step process to narrow thousands of different side hustle ideas down to a small subset matching your interests, abilities, and goals. Specifically, consider and evaluate:
- Various topical areas available to you to focus on for your side hustle
- Ways to flesh out and add substance to your initial topical area
- Various venues or formats through which you can enter the side hustle game
- How your side hustle should relate to your full-time job or career, or possibly other side hustles you already have underway
- Financial considerations on both the money-making and money-spending side of your side hustle
- Time considerations, including the highly valued — and often misunderstood — concept of *passive income*
- Whether you need special skills, training, or licensing for your side hustle
Mash the results of each of these areas together and that overwhelming list of hundreds or thousands of side hustle ideas is narrowed down for you to make your final side hustle decisions.

## Part 3: Putting Your Personal Finances on Firm Footing

### #34 — Categorizing Your Financial Records
Getting organized takes time, but the payoff is no more late payments and fees from a misplaced credit card bill, tax records organized in minutes rather than hours, and critical documents ready at a moment's notice. Four steps:
1. **Gather all your financial documents**, paperwork, bills, and unopened mail.
2. **Set up a filing system** (structure in tip #35).
3. **Separate your paperwork into four stacks**: Bills to Be Paid; To Do/Read; File; and Shred.
4. **Place each stack in its new home** — Bills to Be Paid and To Do/Read into their new files, File items into their appropriate folders, and shred any paper containing personal or private information before recycling.
Every time you retrieve the mail or obtain additional paperwork from work, from the bank, and so on, immediately repeat steps 3 and 4. If in doubt, shred it.

### #35 — Setting Up a Filing System for Your Financial Records
A guide to organizing your personal financial files (paper or digital) — for most, fewer files are necessary; your situation, preferences, or document volume may call for more. This also outlines **how long to retain** each type.
- **Advisors** — names and contact info for the important people in your financial life: banker, physician, attorney, among others.
- **Auto** — *Title*: keep as long as you own the vehicle. *Maintenance records*: keep as long as you own the vehicle.
- **Bank accounts** — checking, savings, money market. Per account: *current monthly statement* (keep until you receive the next statement) and *historic monthly statements* (keep until the accounts reconciled; if you ever need a historic statement for some reason, your bank can provide a copy).
- **Bills due** — upon opening your mail, file bills due in this file. After you pay the bills, the file will be empty again.
- **Contracts** — all legal agreements and employment contracts; keep as long as they're in force.
- **Credit cards** — use one file folder for each account: *current monthly statement* (keep until you reconcile the next statement) and *historic monthly statements* (keep until the account is reconciled; keep at minimum any item under warranty, and if you purchased something that may be a tax-deductible expense, keep the record indefinitely).
- **Credit history** — *credit report*: obtain a copy of your report (and your spouse's) at least annually, retain until you receive a new report, then compare the two. *Debt management action plan*: track debt repayment progress with this scorecard.
- **Education** — enrollment records; diplomas and certificates; grade cards or transcripts; progress reports.
- **Employment** — employee handbook (current); employee benefits handbook (current); paycheck stub (most current only, if cumulative); employee evaluations (all); resume (current); continuing education units (CEU, all required documentation of CEU requirements, if applicable); proof of current licensure, if licenses are required.
- **Healthcare (for informational purposes only)** — medical records; vaccination information; receipts (may be needed for tax return).
- **Home repairs and maintenance** — retain records, invoices, estimates, business cards, and so on for any home services or repairs (plumbing, electric, lawn maintenance) as well as any receipts for equipment you've purchased, for as long as you own the item.
- **Insurance** — retain current copies of personally owned insurance policies (auto, home, boat, life, disability, long-term care, or any other type you have). In addition to your policies, also keep a copy of your most current annual statement.
- **Investments** — *bank investment account statements*. *Brokerage accounts*: although you aren't required by law to keep your account application, consider putting it in this file — hopefully you'll never need it, but you should also keep notes or correspondence from the broker (for as long as you own the asset) and prospectuses (the one issued when the investment was originally made, plus the most current for each investment position held), as well as annual reports (keep the most current for each investment position held) and monthly or quarterly account statements (retain indefinitely). Also file and retain any purchase or sale confirmation statements for at least three years, or until the investment is completely liquidated.
- **Mutual fund statements** — keep account applications, notes and correspondence from your advisor, prospectuses (the one when first purchased and most current), annual report (if cumulative, retain the year-end and cumulative statements as well as each current monthly or quarterly statement), and purchase and sale confirmation statements for at least three years after the investment is completely liquidated.
- **Employer-sponsored retirement plans** — retain your Summary Plan Description, investment options, current account statement (keep until the next one comes), and annual account summary statement.
- **IRAs** — save your current account statement, any annual account summary statements (retain indefinitely), and any records of contributions, both traditional (deductible and nondeductible) and Roth accounts.
- **Other investments** — rental property, limited partnerships, dividend reinvestment plans, and annuities. Anything that doesn't fit into another investment file goes here.
- **Loans** — keep all documents for each loan (mortgage, auto loan, home equity line of credit, signature loan, or school loan) for as long as the loan is outstanding. This documentation includes payments and amortization schedules; keep your paid-in-full verification. After the loan is paid off, financial reading material is a parking place for important material that came in the mail. Then, when you get to a more convenient time, you'll move it to a more appropriate file, an investment account newsletter, which frequently is the recycle bin.
- **Tax records** — hang on to all receipts, 1099s, paycheck stubs, charitable contributions, and other items pertaining to the current year. In most cases you need to keep tax returns and supporting records — the proof behind the numbers on your tax return that you signed and filed — for three years; however, you may keep them indefinitely.
- **To do (pending projects)** — to-do items, such as an estimate for work you may have done on your home or an investment prospectus that you haven't read yet. Retain until completed or dropped.
- **Utilities** — retain current monthly statements for water, gas, electric, cable, phone (both landline and mobile), and streaming. As you file these papers, consider recording on a single piece of paper your monthly costs for each service — take a moment to look at usage trends and you'll likely become more energy-efficient and save money.
- **Warranties** — retain the documentation until the item is no longer under warranty. This includes any whole house, appliance, or electronics warranties.

### #36 — Finding an Insurance Agent
Automobile, home, boat, umbrella, and other personal policies are rarely, if ever, sold off the shelf. Most, if not all, of those major risks need proper coverage — endorsements, coverage limits, and appropriate customizing. That's why for most people, finding and hiring the best agent requires a great deal of care. Build a checklist of what you want in an agent by asking yourself:
- **Do I want my life, health, disability, long-term-care, and other coverages with the same agent?** You'll have the best-designed program if you can find one agent with expertise to oversee your whole program — at the very least, it's wise not to have more than two agents that you work with.
- **Is a regular, yearly review important to me?** If so, add this to your shopping list. Regular reviews are recommended: a well-designed insurance plan starts to rust with coverage gaps if it's not polished up every year or two.
- **Do I have a home business?** If so, you must find someone with small-business insurance expertise.
- **Are top claim skills important to me?** Do you want the best possible claims coaching, to maximize your claim when you file it? Do you want an agent skilled enough to fight successfully for your rights if your claim is unjustly denied or underpaid?
Then use the answers to screen candidates. Sources for candidates — get at least two to three prospects:
- **Word of mouth** is always one of the best sources when seeking a professional of any kind. But be careful not to fall into the *price trap*: because so many people buy their insurance solely on price, when you ask for a referral for a good agent, you might get "Call Bob. He's a good guy. He saved me $200 a year. And he always remembers my birthday." You call Bob, get his quote, save your $200 or more, and end up with a good price for the wrong coverage (and an annual birthday card) — and you've done nothing about your uninsured coverage gaps. You don't necessarily want the best *salesperson*, you want the person who will give you the best professional *advice*.
- **Professional societies** — an insurance agent can earn advanced designations by completing a series of courses and passing exams: Chartered Property Casualty Underwriter (CPCU), Certified Life Underwriter (CLU), Accredited Advisor in Insurance (AAI). If you want good leads to an agent prospect with expertise in personal property and liability policies — home, umbrella, and so on — contact the CPCU Society (`cpcusociety.org`) and The National Alliance for Insurance Education and Research (`scic.com`). Whatever names you get from these two societies will be good prospects for your agent search.
- **Insurance companies** — if you're already insured with a particular company you want to be insured with, go directly to the insurance company for agent referrals. You can also go to the insurance company for agent leads if you've shopped ahead for a certain type of insurance and found one or two insurers that are the lowest priced. (All you know at this point is that they're the lowest priced for the coverage you shopped, not necessarily the best.)
What you need to find out from the insurer is who the company's best, most knowledgeable agents are. The company is unlikely for legal and other reasons to give you their names, so try this: call the local company office and ask them to email you a list of all their agents in your state who have a CPCU or CIC designation. They may not have a list at their fingertips, but they can get it for you. Whichever method you use, it should yield a small supply of quality prospects.

At this point you've narrowed down your choice to one or two candidates, probably thinking, "How do I, with limited knowledge, make this choice? I don't even know what to ask." Start by requesting a face-to-face meeting for the purpose of doing an insurance review — for every policy that you have, including your group coverage at work. You'll be able to tell by your gut feel whether this is the person for you. The agent with the greater expertise and greater care for your well-being will stand out. If you've narrowed your field to two candidates, have both of them do an insurance review.

### #37 — Having a Balanced Insurance Program
A great insurance program has two key components:
- **Your program is in balance in all major risk areas**: major damage to or destruction of your residence; major lawsuits; premature death; long-term disability; major medical bills.
- **Each policy that you buy is well-designed with high limits for major loss coverages and with all the right endorsements that customize your policy to cover the risks in your life that would not otherwise be covered.**
You'll have a better chance of accomplishing both goals if you take time to locate a highly skilled personal insurance agent who's an expert on every type of personal insurance that you need (see tip #36).

Yes, the cost of insurance is important — you want competitive and manageable premiums. But the true cost of your insurance program is not only what you pay upfront in premiums but, more importantly, what you have to pay out-of-pocket at claim time. Most people who shop for insurance put all the emphasis on the front-end costs — the premiums — and then may end up having to pay thousands or hundreds of thousands of dollars in uncovered claims later. **When it comes to insurance costs, it's far better to pay a little too much in premiums than to pay for an uncovered major claim later.**

Many people have major loss coverage that's out of balance. They may have a good medical plan with high limits, but no coverage for long-term disabilities. They may have $1 million of life insurance on the breadwinner, but none on the homemaker. Their home may be fully insured, but they have only $100,000 of coverage for lawsuits and no umbrella liability policy. You face several major risks regularly throughout your lifetime that, if they occur, can cause your financial ruin: major medical bills, major damage to or destruction of your residence, major lawsuits, premature death, long-term disability, and — especially for those older than age 40 — the risk of extended long-term care. Your insurance program is in balance if each of these major risk areas is equally well covered and you're not spending too much on one area and too little on another.

A highly skilled agent can help you identify imbalances in your insurance program and suggest the corrective action needed, which is why taking the time to find the right agent is so important. Most people who buy insurance don't take the time to find the right agent. They let whoever answered the phone and gave them the quote be their agent, without any knowledge of that person's skill level. And in the end, they get a less-skilled agent than they could have had for the same price.

### #38 — Deciding between Permanent and Term Life Insurance
There are really only two types of life insurance — **permanent** and **term** — although the two types come in many shapes, sizes, and colors. The biggest difference between them is how long the coverage lasts:
- **Permanent life insurance** covers you for your entire life. Your death is certain, and when you die, it pays the death benefit (the amount of money payable at the time of death).
- **Term life insurance** covers only a part of your lifetime. When that part, or term, ends, so does the coverage. It only pays a death benefit if you die within the designated term.

**Permanent life insurance** is ideally suited to permanent needs — for example, you may buy permanent life insurance for your surviving spouse, covering estate taxes due upon your death, or paying final expenses (burial, legal costs, and so on). Every permanent life insurance policy has two core parts to its price:
- **Mortality cost** — determined by your odds of dying at that moment. The mortality charge increases each year as you age and your risk of dying increases.
- **Policy expense cost** — your share of insurance company expenses (rent, staff, and agent commissions). The expense charge stays relatively constant.
Most permanent life insurance policies have level premiums for life. How is that possible if the mortality charge increases every year? The insurance company averages the increasing mortality changes over your remaining expected life. In short, you overpay in the early years so that you can underpay in the later years. That overpayment is set aside in a reserve for you, called **cash value**. If you cancel a permanent policy, you're entitled to get back much of those overpayments — the cash value. The cash value is minimal in the first couple of years because of heavy first-year costs — underwriting, medical exams, and agent commissions.

**Term life insurance** is ideally suited for covering life insurance needs that aren't permanent. For example, you may buy term life insurance when you want to cover college costs for children while the kids are growing up. Term life insurance costs, unlike permanent life insurance, increase regularly as you age. Sometimes it's every five years or more, and sometimes the increase is annual. Term life insurance costs can be averaged over ten years for the entire term — if you drop a term insurance policy in the first several years, you get no refund of any overpayment, so the price is considerably lower than permanent insurance premiums for the same death benefit. Because term life insurance has no cash value element, term insurance doesn't have a cash value.

### #39 — Choosing a Permanent Life Insurance Policy
With permanent life insurance, the cost is substantially higher in the first several years of your coverage. However, the cost can't go up: the initial premium rate is as bad as it'll ever get, and the insurance company can't deny benefits as long as you pay your premiums on time. So if you need life insurance coverage indefinitely — for example, if you need cash available for your heirs to pay estate or inheritance taxes upon your death — you're a good candidate for permanent life insurance. Permanent insurance comes in two primary flavors:
- **Universal**: you may need a lot of life insurance now and well into your retirement years. If that's the case, universal life insurance is likely the right type of insurance for at least a portion of your life insurance need. This type of life insurance has an investment as well as an insurance component. Your initial premiums are applied to purchase life insurance. Then, additional premiums either accumulate in a fixed account (universal life) or are invested (variable universal life) to provide more growth (and volatility) opportunities, much like stock and bond mutual funds.
- **Whole**: if you need coverage for your entire life, no matter how long you live, whole life insurance may be the right option for that portion of your life insurance need.
**Warning:** insurance agents make a lot more money selling universal, variable universal life, and whole life insurance than they do selling term insurance. This inherent conflict of interest is something that you need to always keep in mind when considering the recommendations of an insurance agent or financial advisor who sells life insurance.

### #40 — Selecting a Term Life Insurance Policy
Like home or auto insurance, term insurance is pure insurance in that it pays off only if you make a claim. The cost of the insurance can go up every year (*annual renewable term*) or remain level for a specific number of years (for example, 5, 10, 20, or 30 years). You can lose your insurance coverage if you fail to pay the premiums when they're due, if the term expires and the policy isn't guaranteed renewable, or if you elect not to renew. If you have that option, term insurance is by far the cheapest form of life insurance. A few suggestions:
- **Twenty-year level term coverage** is generally an excellent option for families with young children, whether or not they need a lot or a little insurance, because the insurance will be there to support the surviving family, at least until the kids are grown.
- **Consider level premium term life insurance coverage.** For one thing, the level premium guarantees that you know exactly how much your insurance will cost for a set number of years. And level premium term life insurance can be extremely cost-effective. Consider using level premium term insurance with a guaranteed level premium for the number of years that you anticipate needing the coverage, and also consider adding the guaranteed renewable rider.
- **A guaranteed renewable rider** is a very attractive feature available with many term insurance policies. It assures that you'll be able to retain a term policy at the end of its initial term — if you still need the insurance — simply by paying the premiums in effect at that time. You don't have to reapply or provide evidence of insurability. This provision provides great flexibility when you're not exactly sure when you'll no longer need coverage.
- **Annual renewable term (ART)**, if you need coverage for only five to ten years at most, tends to be less expensive for the first five or six years of the policy compared to a 10- or 20-year level term policy. But every year the premiums continue to increase, and the policy that was once very attractive becomes more expensive than a level term policy. The break-even point is so early in the life of the ART policy that buying this coverage makes no sense unless you won't need it very long. In every other situation where term insurance would be appropriate, go with the level premium term insurance.
- Many people need at least **$200,000 worth of life insurance**, or several times that amount, to provide for their surviving family in the event of premature death, but they can't afford to spend thousands of dollars per year on life insurance premiums. They need life insurance, but they only need the coverage for a limited period of time — so in this case, term insurance is the best fit.

## TL;DR

When money's tight: figure out your real net worth and true spendable income first, separate required expenses from discretionary ones so you know exactly where the fat is, track spending for real (not from memory), and turn vague financial worries into a concrete budget and action plan. Manage the stress side deliberately — breathing, progressive relaxation, staying organized, and leaning on your support system all measurably help. If income needs to go up, treat your resume as a living "core" document you customize per job, use keywords and results (not vague duty statements) to stand out, over-prepare for interviews without sounding scripted, follow up with an influencing letter instead of a generic thank-you, and use call-email-call plus your network (phone, not just text) to surface opportunities faster than job boards alone. Beyond the current job: consider stable federal work (USAJOBS.gov), retraining into growing fields, or telecommuting — and if you go independent, honestly vet whether you're self-starter material, then set up a home-based business properly (business plan, pros, legal structure, licenses, insurance, accounting, marketing) with eyes open to the health-insurance and bankruptcy risks.

Once you're running your own thing: time the exit from your old job around vesting, bonuses, and health procedures; start part-time and turn your old employer into your first client; take every deduction you're legally owed (home office, vehicle, family hires, retirement plans) with an accountant's help; and build a three-to-twelve-month cash reserve before you buy the fun stuff. In a downturn, positive cash flow beats everything — stay glued to your existing customers, chase receivables the day they're late, cut expenses before chasing revenue, run a time-boxed promotion, and refuse to give up, since persistence is what successful entrepreneurs credit most. If cutting expenses isn't enough, add income with a side hustle chosen methodically (topic, format, relation to your day job, money in/out, time and passive income, required skills or licensing) rather than picked off a list of a thousand ideas.

On the paperwork-and-protection side: get organized once (gather everything, file it, four stacks — pay, read, file, shred) and the late fees and tax scrambles stop. Keep a real retention schedule rather than hoarding or tossing at random. For insurance, the front-end premium is the least important number — the true cost is what you pay out-of-pocket at claim time, so buy a *balanced* program covering all five ruin-level risks (residence, lawsuits, premature death, long-term disability, major medical, plus long-term care after 40) and find one genuinely skilled agent rather than the cheapest quote or whoever answered the phone. On life insurance: permanent for permanent needs (estate taxes, final expenses), term for temporary ones (kids until they're grown) — term is far cheaper, 20-year level premium suits young families, add a guaranteed renewable rider when you're unsure how long you'll need it, and remember agents earn considerably more selling permanent policies than term.
